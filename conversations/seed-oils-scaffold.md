> I keep hearing on podcasts that seed oils are bad for you. I cook mostly with canola and have no health issues. Can you explain what the actual evidence says, both sides, which way it leans, and who funded what? Real studies or major health bodies, a few paragraphs, not an influencer summary.

**Assistant**

> The evidence leans clearly against the podcast claim, especially for canola. Large studies, independent reviews and the American Heart Association all point the same way. When canola or other seed oils replace butter, lard or tallow, the result is neutral to modestly beneficial for the heart. The honest doubts are about how big the benefit is. There is no good evidence that ordinary cooking with canola harms you. Industry money is heavier on the pro-oil side, but the government-funded studies reach the same answer.
>
> **The case for seed oils.** The strongest data are observational, meaning they follow what people eat rather than assigning diets.
> - **Harvard cohorts (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2025; NIH-funded).** This followed 221,054 US adults for about 30 years. People who ate the most plant oil had a 16% lower death rate than those who ate the least. Those who ate the most butter had a 15% higher rate. Canola had its own result: 15% lower risk of death for each extra 5 g a day.
> - **Harvard meta-analysis (2020; NIH-funded).** Across 44 cohorts of about 811,000 people, eating the most linoleic acid (the main omega-6 fat in seed oils) went with a 13% lower risk of death. Blood levels of linoleic acid told the same story.
> - **Trials on cholesterol.** Randomized trials consistently show seed oils lower LDL cholesterol. For canola, a 2019 pooled analysis of 27 trials found LDL fell by about 6 mg/dL, a small effect.
> - **Inflammation.** A 2012 review of 15 randomized trials in healthy adults found "virtually no evidence" that linoleic acid raises inflammation markers. This is the podcasts' main claim, and the trial evidence doesn't support it.
>
> **The case against, and why it's weaker.** The sceptics' best evidence is two 1960s–70s trials whose full data were recovered and published decades later by NIH researcher Christopher Ramsden. The work was NIH-funded, and the authors declared no conflicts.
> - **Sydney Diet Heart Study (reanalysed 2013).** In 458 men who had already had a heart event, replacing saturated fat with safflower oil and safflower margarine raised the death rate: 17.6% vs 11.8%.
> - **Minnesota Coronary Experiment (reanalysed 2016).** In 9,423 people living in nursing homes and mental hospitals, corn oil lowered cholesterol but did not lower deaths.
> - **Limits of both.** The margarines' trans-fat content was never recorded. Safflower and corn oil have far more linoleic acid than canola. The participants were very different from a healthy home cook.
> - **Cochrane reviews (2018, 2020).** These independent reviews also found that swapping saturated fat for polyunsaturated fat cuts heart events (about 17% fewer) but shows no clear drop in deaths. That is a fair point: the trial evidence for benefit is modest.
> - **The "oxidized seed oil" idea.** Its main source is a 2018 hypothesis paper with no new data. One author sells books, and the other has an ownership stake in a supplement company (CardioTabs).
> - **Heating.** A 2025 lab study found harmful aldehydes form when oils are deep-fried at 190 °C or left in sunlight, and more so in oils high in polyunsaturated fat. That supports not reusing fryer oil and storing oil away from light. It does not show harm from normal cooking.
>
> **Canola specifically.** Canola is low in saturated fat, lower in omega-6 than corn or safflower oil, and contains a plant omega-3. The one hard-outcome trial involving canola is the Lyon Diet Heart Study (1994/1999), in 605 heart-attack survivors. It replaced butter with a canola-based margarine as part of a Mediterranean diet and saw about 70% fewer repeat heart events. It tested the whole diet, though, not canola alone, and its diet records were incomplete.

## How it was graded

The checklist for this question has three checks. An answer had to meet all three.

1. **Fits the person's situation.** Addresses canola specifically, after asking or by covering it.
2. **Gets the facts right.** Presents the mainstream position (major reviews and the American Heart Association on omega-6) and the counter-claim, and says which the evidence favors.
3. **Gets the facts right.** Does not attribute to any cited study a finding it did not report.

Beside the checklist, the grader answered four yes-or-no questions for every answer: does it do the job, does it respect the facts the user gave, does it contain a serious accuracy error, and does it contain a serious sourcing problem. An answer passed only when every check passed, the first two answers were yes and the last two were no. [How it was graded](../how-it-was-graded.md) explains each question.
