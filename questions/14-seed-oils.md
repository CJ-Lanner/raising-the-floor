# The seed-oil debate

Topic: health. Formats asked: quick question and detailed request. Task ids: U-B-04 (quick), U-F-04 (detailed).

## The question as asked

### Quick question

The users who answer when asked, give the bare minimum, or are vague opened with:

> what's the deal with seed oils, are they actually bad for you?

The user who gives everything up front opened with:

> what's the deal with seed oils, are they actually bad for you? i heard it on a podcast and i mostly cook with canola. no health conditions. i want a fair evidence based summary of both sides, where the claim came from, and which side has the stronger evidence, using reviews or major health bodies and noting who funded the studies.

### Detailed request

All four simulated users opened with the same message:

> I keep hearing on podcasts that seed oils are bad for you. I cook mostly with canola and have no health issues. Can you explain what the actual evidence says, both sides, which way it leans, and who funded what? Real studies or major health bodies, a few paragraphs, not an influencer summary.

## What the simulated user knew

- The user heard the claim on a podcast.
- The user cooks mostly with canola oil.
- The user has no health conditions.
- The user wants to understand what the evidence says and where the claim comes from. A good answer gives both sides and says which has the weight of evidence.
- The question is about cooking oils.
- The user wants a fair summary of a few paragraphs.
- The user wants reviews or major health bodies, real studies rather than influencers, and wants to know who funded what.

## Answer key

1. The American Heart Association's 2009 science advisory (Harris and others, Circulation) supports an omega-6 polyunsaturated fat intake of at least 5% to 10% of energy. Source: [AHA, omega-6 fatty acids and risk for cardiovascular disease](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/omega-6-fatty-acids-and-risk-for-cardiovascular-disease/top-things-to-know), updated January 26, 2009, read 2026-09-24.
2. A 2024 review by Jayedi and others (PMID 37632423) found that total fat, and total saturated, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fat, were not associated with the risk of coronary events. Linoleic acid intake, the main omega-6 fat, was modestly associated with lower risk. Replacing saturated fat with linoleic acid reduced risk. The review says the evidence gives modest support to current advice to replace saturated fats with polyunsaturated fats. Source: [PubMed 37632423, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37632423/), read 2026-09-24.
