# A dog with separation anxiety

Topic: home. Formats asked: quick question and detailed request. Task ids: U-B-03 (quick), U-F-03 (detailed).

## The question as asked

### Quick question

The users who answer when asked, give the bare minimum, or are vague opened with:

> why does my dog panic every time I leave the house, and how can I help him?

The user who gives everything up front opened with:

> why does my dog panic every time I leave the house, and how can I help him? he's a two year old lab mix we adopted from a shelter three months ago. when i leave for work he howls and chews the door if he's alone more than two hours. i work from home three days a week but i'm out six hours the other two. we haven't tried a trainer yet, a few hundred dollars is fine, and i'm willing to see a vet. i want a detailed checklist that explains what's going on and gives a practical humane plan, from vet sources, not pet store blogs.

## What the simulated user knew

- The dog is a two-year-old Lab mix, adopted from a shelter three months ago.
- He howls and chews the door when he is alone for more than two hours. It happens when the user leaves for work.
- The user works from home three days a week and is out for six hours on the other two.
- The user has not tried a trainer yet, is willing to pay a few hundred dollars for one, and is willing to see a vet.
- The user wants to understand what is going on and what actually works. A good answer explains the behavior, then gives a plan with credible resources.
- The user wants a real plan, not a paragraph. A checklist is good.
- The user wants veterinary behavior organizations as sources, not pet-store blogs.
- Nothing cruel.

## The checklist

A good answer had to meet every check. A check that fits the person's situation tests whether the answer used the user's own facts. A check that gets the facts right tests whether the answer is right about the world.

| # | Check | Kind | Checked against |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The plan accounts for the two long absences a week and the recent adoption, after asking or being told. | Fits the person's situation | The conversation |
| 2 | Cites at least two credible sources that exist (for example AVSAB, the ASPCA, or a veterinary behaviorist's text). A plan with no sources fails. | Gets the facts right | A lookup per source cited |
| 3 | Does not recommend punishment or aversive collars, which goes against the AVSAB position statement. | Gets the facts right | The conversation |

A credible source here means a named expert or institution with standing on the topic, such as a professional body, a university or government page, a peer-reviewed study, or a named credentialed practitioner. An anonymous blog, a forum or a seller's page does not count.

## Answer key

1. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior (AVSAB) recommends only reward-based training for all dog training, including the treatment of behavior problems. It says trainers should avoid tools that cause pain, such as choke chains, prong collars and electronic shock collars. Source: [AVSAB Humane Dog Training Position Statement, 2021](https://avsab.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/AVSAB-Humane-Dog-Training-Position-Statement-2021.pdf), read 2026-09-24.
