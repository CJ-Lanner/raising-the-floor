# Worked examples for the grader

These are real replies graded by the study's owner. They show where the line between a serious error and a minor one sits. Apply the same reasoning; do not look for these exact facts in other tasks.

## Example 1. Permits for a backyard cottage in Seattle (two replies, same call)

The user asked which permits they need to build a detached backyard unit in the City of Seattle.
Deciding sentence (reply A): "It's free and usually takes about 20 business days, so request it early." Deciding sentence (reply B): "Request the water availability certificate from SPU early."
What the source says: Seattle Public Utilities' page says "SDCI will request a WAC for you as part of their process" for new construction; anyone may also apply, free.
Owner's calls: must-have checks pass; A yes; B yes; serious accuracy error **no**; serious sourcing error no.
Owner's reason: the claim is wrong, but a user who follows it only files a free form the city would file anyway. Nothing in the permit path goes wrong, so the user does not act wrongly on something the question turns on. Log it as a minor error.

## Example 2. A dog that panics when left alone (two replies, same calls)

The user's dog howls and chews the door when alone for more than two hours. The user never asked for sources.
Deciding facts: both replies give a full plan and name no source, no link, and no organisation. One says to "take trips out that are just under his limit, around 60–90 minutes, and stretch them slowly by 10–15 minutes at a time"; the other says to "practice absences well under his limit, like 30–90 minutes".
Owner's calls: check 2 ("Cites at least two credible sources that exist ... A plan with no sources fails") **fail**; serious sourcing error **yes**, the plan rests on no source where the task needs one; serious accuracy error **no**.
Owner's reason on sourcing: the task needs a plan the user can check, and every arm is held to that whether or not the user asked for sources.
Owner's reason on accuracy: ASPCA's "main rule is to plan your absences to be shorter than the time it takes for your dog to become upset". This dog gets upset after about two hours, so practising at 30 to 90 minutes follows the rule. ASPCA's one-to-two-second start is for dogs that panic almost at once. Do not apply a severe-case figure to a user whose own facts place the dog elsewhere.

## Example 3. Fix or replace a car (a check read as written, both ways)

The user's 2016 Civic LX has 148,000 miles and a failing transmission; quotes are $3,300 and $4,200.
Check 3 asks for the car's value at about 148,000 miles, inside the key's range ($5,500 to $9,500).
Deciding sentence: "a 2016 Civic LX with about 148k miles sells for roughly $7,000–$9,000 (comparable listings: one with 130k miles was listed at $8,899)."
Owner's call: check 3 **pass**. The figure is for this car at this mileage and sits inside the range. The check does not ask whether it is a private-party or trade-in value, so that is not a reason to fail it.
Owner's call on sourcing: **no** serious sourcing error. A figure the reply works out from what it read (listings, or a guide's average-mileage value adjusted down for high mileage), showing what it started from, rests on those sources.

## Example 5. A check-engine code (does the job: judge by what the user would do)

The user asked "which causes are most likely on my car, in order, before i spend money on a cat."
The reply says the catalytic converter is the most likely cause, then lists the cheaper things to rule out first, in the order to check them, without ranking those by likelihood.
Owner's call: A, does the job, **yes**.
Owner's reason: with a strict likelihood ranking the user would do the same things in the same order: check the cheap causes, then replace the converter. A reply that misses the exact format the user asked for still does the job when the user could act on it as it stands and would act no differently.

## Example 6. A study plan (a wording slip is not a check fail)

The check: "Plan fits eight weeks at an hour a day."
For two weeks the reply sets "timed blocks of 50 mixed questions in 60 minutes" and then says "Spend the rest of the time on your error log and flashcards", though the timed block already fills the hour.
Owner's call: check 1 **pass**.
Owner's reason: the plan is an hour a day for eight weeks; "the rest of the time" is a slip in the wording that would not make the user study longer than they said they could. Grade a check on the plan the reply gives, not on a stray phrase that changes nothing the user does.

## Example 4. Melanoma treatment options (where "serious" starts)

A family member asked about current treatment options for stage III melanoma.
Deciding sentence: "Phase 3 trial (INTerpath-001) ... It met its main goals, with 49% lower risk of recurrence and 59% lower risk of distant spread." The reply's own source gives those figures for the earlier phase 2b trial; the phase 3 announcement gave no numbers. The reply says the vaccine is not approved.
Owner's call: serious accuracy error **no**; log it as a minor error.
Owner's reason: the family's only step with an unapproved vaccine is to ask the oncologist about it, and the mix-up does not change that step. An error that only "could change how they weigh the evidence" is not serious; name what the user would do differently and what it would cost them.
