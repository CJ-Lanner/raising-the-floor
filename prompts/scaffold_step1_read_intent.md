Here is a request someone typed to an assistant: [first message]

Read it and fill in the form below. Do not answer the request.

**What kind of question is it?** Pick one.
- find: the person wants one thing that already exists, such as a number, a date, a rule, a price, or a list. A single line or list would be a complete answer.
- understand: the person wants to know how or why something is the way it is. A good answer gathers evidence, weighs it, and often has more than one side.
- decide: the person wants a pick among options that exist, made for their situation. Without knowing their situation there is no right answer.

**What did they tell you, and what is missing?** Four things shape every answer. For each one, say whether the request states it (give the value you read) or leaves it out.
- goal: what the answer is for and what a good one looks like to them. "A stroller" is a topic, not a goal. "One stroller I can carry up three flights and take on planes" is a goal.
- scope: which slice of the topic they mean: the place, the time period, the particular kind. "Is it safe to swim" has no scope. "Is it safe to swim in Lake Michigan near Chicago in early June" does.
- depth: how much they want. One line, a short list, a full explanation.
- source_preference: what kind of source they would trust. Official sites, independent tests, peer-reviewed studies, or no preference.

**Which missing things would change your answer?** List only those, most important first, as load_bearing_missing. The test: if two reasonable people gave different values, would you give them different answers? For "what running shoes should I buy", budget and the surface they run on change the pick, so both are load-bearing. For "when was the Eiffel Tower finished", nothing missing changes the answer, so the list is empty.

**Four more things matter only for some requests.** List the ones that matter for this request as conditional, so that only those get asked about.
- budget: a spending limit.
- recency: how current the information has to be.
- must_include_or_avoid: something the answer has to contain or stay away from. "It has to work on a Mac." "Nothing with a subscription."
- format: the form they want the answer in. One pick and a backup, a table, a day by day list.

**stakes**: low, medium, or high. What happens to this person if the answer is wrong. Money, health, legal matters, or anything hard to undo means medium or high. If the person says it matters a lot to them, take their word.

**research_needed**: little, some, or a lot. How much digging the question needs, whatever the stakes and however short the answer should be. little: one good source settles it. some: a few sources have to be compared. a lot: many sources, conflicting evidence, or several sub-questions that each need their own search. A curious reader asking what the latest research says about a disease has low stakes and needs a lot.

**topic_flags**: any of these that apply. Each one changes how the later steps work and what the final answer has to tell the person.
- contested: informed people disagree. Later steps look for both sides, and the answer shows them and says which way the evidence leans.
- fast_moving: the right answer changed in the last year or could change soon. Later steps check the current status, and the answer dates its facts.
- location_dependent: the answer differs by place. If the place is not known it gets asked for, and the answer says which place it is for.

**assumptions**: for each missing thing that is not load-bearing, the assumption you would state instead of asking.
